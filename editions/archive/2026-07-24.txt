FIELD-GUIDE FRIDAY

The Cape May–Lewes Ferry. You've booked the 4:15 PM sailing on Wednesday, August 19 — the vehicle slot is paid, the crossing is 85 minutes, and the check-in window closes at 3:45 sharp. Miss it and you forfeit the fare plus a $26 no-show fee. The ferry matters because it's the only dignified way to claim Delaware without backtracking through Philadelphia, and because the 17-mile crossing of Delaware Bay at golden hour — with a real chance of dolphins off the beam — is one of the trip's unscripted gifts. Arrive at the Lewes terminal by 3:15. You'll need the same photo IDs you used that morning at the Naval Academy's Gate 1. The ferry lands you in Cape May at sunset, and the state count goes to five.

THE CULTURE FILE

Cape May is the nation's oldest seaside resort — Congress was already vacationing there in the 1790s. The entire downtown is a National Historic Landmark District, the only one in the country devoted to a resort city, and the Victorian gingerbread you'll see on every block isn't replica charm: it's original construction from the 1880s rebuilding after the Great Fire of 1878. Cape May's other gift is location — it sits at the literal southern tip of New Jersey, farther south than Baltimore, which is why the light feels different and the beach faces southeast into open Atlantic. Presidents Grant, Harrison, Buchanan, and Pierce all summered there. The Victorian aesthetic survived because the town never tore anything down to modernize — the resort quietly declined through the mid-20th century, preservationists arrived in the 1970s, and now the whole place is a pastel postcard suspended in 1895.
