THE POSTCARD

"There are few hours in life more agreeable than the hour dedicated to the ceremony known as afternoon tea." — Henry James, who summered in Newport and set the opening of *The Portrait of a Lady* there. The town gave him Bellevue Avenue and its parade of gilded excess; he gave literature Isabel Archer walking those same sidewalks in 1880, deciding what kind of life she wanted. You'll walk the Cliff Walk on August 21 — same view, 146 years later, infinitely better company.

THE CULTURE FILE

Newport claims 15 of the 25 finest Gilded Age mansions still standing in America, most built in a 20-year sprint (1885–1905) when "cottage" meant 70 rooms and a private power plant. The Breakers alone required 2,500 workers and cost $7 million in 1895 dollars — about $265 million today, for a summer house the Vanderbilts used ten weeks a year. James watched it all with a novelist's eye: prosperity as spectacle, architecture as theater, excess as the great American art form. Your reservation at The Wayfinder puts you in the heart of the old harbor district, where the fishing boats that fed those banquets once tied up at Bowen's Wharf.
