TRIVIA THURSDAY

The Cape May–Lewes Ferry has been crossing Delaware Bay since 1964, but the waterway itself has ferried history for centuries longer. When Captain Cornelius Jacobsen Mey sailed these waters in 1620, he claimed the cape for the Dutch West India Company and lent it his name — albeit in mangled English spelling. The bay he mapped would later become the front line of oyster wars, rumrunner routes during Prohibition, and a critical shipping lane that still funnels 40% of the Port of Philadelphia's cargo. Your crossing on August 19th takes 85 minutes, which is just long enough to stand on deck and watch the coastline dissolve, then reform as something new. Dolphins ride the ferry's wake about half the time — they treat the boat like a moving current, effortless and unbothered.

THE CULTURE FILE

Cape May is the nation's oldest seaside resort, drawing vacationers since the 1760s — before there was a nation to vacation from. Its Victorian architecture survived because the town went broke at exactly the right moment: too poor to demolish, too pretty to ignore, it became a National Historic Landmark in 1976. Filmmaker John Waters used Cape May as a stand-in for 1960s Baltimore in *Hairspray* — the pastel rowhouses and old-money porches doubled as Charm City without alteration. And the ferry itself appears in Tom Clancy's *The Hunt for Red October*: Captain Ramius contemplates American freedom while imagining "a ferry across Delaware Bay, where a man could simply buy a ticket and go."
