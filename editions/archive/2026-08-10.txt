WHAT ASHEVILLE HOLDS

You know the headline: Biltmore, 250 rooms, America's largest privately-owned home, your 10:45 AM entry locked for Sunday the 16th. But here's what the estate doesn't advertise as loudly: George Vanderbilt's library held 10,000 volumes in eight languages, the prints and maps room ran its own card catalog, and the original electric Otis elevators still work. He built it as a working retreat — the kind of house where you could disappear into a book in a different room every day for a month. The grounds: Frederick Law Olmsted's last great commission, the same hand that shaped Central Park and the grounds of the 1893 Chicago World's Fair. And the Blue Ridge backdrop? The reason Vanderbilt chose this valley in the first place — on a clear day the ridgeline runs unbroken for 20 miles in either direction.

"The mountains are calling and I must go." — John Muir (1873, in a letter from Yosemite, though the Appalachians answered the same call)

THE CULTURE FILE

Biltmore opened on Christmas Eve 1895 with a house-warming that became legend: Henry James attended, Edith Wharton visited later and borrowed the library's scale for descriptions in "The House of Mirth," and the basement included America's first private bowling alley and a heated indoor pool — Olympic length, filled and heated by the estate's own infrastructure. Asheville itself bloomed in the 1920s as a resort town for the same New York money that built the Breakers in Newport; the Art Deco downtown still shows the silhouette of that Jazz Age summer. And Thomas Wolfe — "Look Homeward, Angel" — grew up here, in a boarding house his mother ran six blocks from Pack Square. His novel put Asheville on the literary map the way Steinbeck claimed the Salinas Valley and Faulkner owned Yoknapatawpha County.
