CHARLOTTESVILLE + ANNAPOLIS WEEK

This week's spotlight: the twin anchors of Day 3 and Day 4. Charlottesville gives you Jefferson's architecture and the Lawn at dusk — that long sweep of colonnades and pavilions he designed for the University of Virginia, still breathtaking two centuries later. Annapolis hands you the Naval Academy's parade grounds, the City Dock's boat-watch, and a crab feast at Boatyard Bar & Grill where the Chesapeake does what it does best.

Virginia's claim comes with Monticello in the morning and the Rotunda in the evening. Maryland's arrives the next night, bracketed by midshipmen and blue crabs.

"I think this is the most beautiful country I have ever seen in any part of the world." — Thomas Jefferson, writing about the view from Monticello in 1826. He spent forty years building that house and never stopped revising it. The Behind the Scenes tour shows you the obsessive genius of it — the clock that tells the day of the week, the dumbwaiter hidden in the fireplace, the bed alcove that opens to both his study and his sitting room. It's architecture as autobiography.

THE CULTURE FILE

Annapolis served as the United States capital from November 1783 to August 1784 — the brief season when the Continental Congress met in the Maryland State House and George Washington resigned his commission in that very building. You can still stand in the room where it happened. The Naval Academy's Gate 1 entrance requires photo ID, so bring both driver's licenses. The Yard opens to visitors until 5:00 PM most days, and the walk from Gate 1 to Bancroft Hall and back takes about ninety minutes if you linger.
