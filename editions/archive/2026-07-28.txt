"I never was on the dull, tame shore but I loved the great sea more and more." — Barry Cornwall (the pen name of Bryan Waller Procter, 1787–1874), from "The Sea," a Victorian ode still carved into lighthouses and coastal inn guestbooks across New England.

THE CULTURE FILE

Portland's working waterfront has anchored the Old Port since 1633 — one of the oldest continuously-operating harbors in America. The neighborhood burned to bedrock in the Great Fire of 1866; the brick-and-granite rebuild rose in defiance, and those same buildings now hold the restaurants you're circling on the map. Winslow Homer spent his final 27 years painting the Maine coast from his Prouts Neck studio, fifteen minutes south — waves, rock, light, and the kind of solitude that makes a person prolific. And if you're wondering why Portland feels like it invented American food culture in 2004, it's because it sort of did: the farm-to-table wave that reshaped a generation of menus had early momentum here, Fore Street leading the charge.
